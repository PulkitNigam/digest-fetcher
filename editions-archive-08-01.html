The Big One

Seven days, four major models — and the largest open-weight release in history

The week of 24 July to 1 August produced an unusual cluster of frontier releases:

ModelLabDateWhat matters
Kimi K3Moonshot AI (China)27 Jul2.8 trillion parameters, ~1.4TB of weights at MXFP4 quantisation. Largest open-weight release ever shipped.
Claude Opus 5Anthropic24 Jul1M-token context. $5/$25 per million tokens standard, $10/$50 fast mode. Scored 43.3% on FrontierBench v0.1.
InklingThinking MachinesLate Jul975B total / 41B active MoE, pretrained on 45 trillion tokens of text, images, audio and video. Apache 2.0.
Ling-3.0-flashAnt Group (China)Late Jul124B total / 5.1B active — an efficiency play. Free until 3 August, weights promised after.

Why the framing everyone used is wrong → Coverage treated these as four separate product launches. Read together they show something else: the open-weight frontier is now being set by Chinese labs and independent startups, not US incumbents. Kimi K3 and Ling-3.0 are Chinese. Inkling is Apache 2.0 from a startup. The two largest genuinely open releases of the week came from outside the American majors entirely — and Meta, historically the US open-weight champion, is conspicuously absent from both this list and the government framework we've been tracking.

The parameter counts deserve a caveat: 2.8T total parameters at 1.4TB is not something you run at home, and "open weights" at that scale means open to well-resourced labs and clouds, not to individuals. Openness is becoming a spectrum defined by who can afford the hardware.

The World

Russia struck Kyiv overnight; at least nine dead

Russian strikes hit five districts of Kyiv overnight, killing at least nine and wounding 27, including four children, according to local officials — hours after the mayor had warned of an incoming ballistic missile attack. Seven were killed and 14 wounded in Darnytsky district alone; two killed and eight wounded in Solomiansky, where a five-storey residential building was hit.

Context that matters: the UN found June was the deadliest month for Ukrainian civilians since April 2022. Both sides have escalated, with Moscow substantially increasing long-range strikes on cities.

Connecting to yesterday → We noted the Rubio–Lavrov meeting in Manila lasted 35 minutes and produced nothing. This is what "produced nothing" looks like eight days later. Diplomatic failure isn't an abstraction with a lag — it has a body count, and the interval between the two is short.

Iran: strikes may run for days

The Wall Street Journal reports, citing sources, that new US strikes on Iran could last several days. Iran has accused the US of "escalating tensions" amid reports of plans to hit energy sites. Against the standing Hormuz blockade we've tracked since edition 001, striking energy infrastructure while a chokepoint is already closed is the escalation path with the widest economic blast radius.

FIFA scraps the World Cup privatisation plan

Gianni Infantino has abandoned the "FIFA Forward Enterprise" project, which would have sold stakes in the World Cup to private investors including Joshua Kushner. It collapsed under a European boycott threat and internal FIFA opposition. Rare and worth noting: institutional pushback actually working.

Money

Bitcoin under $63,000 as the US crypto bill stalls

Bitcoin fell below $63,000 on Friday, with fading momentum behind US market-structure legislation and weak Coinbase earnings pushing speculative money out of risk. Senate Republicans released a fresh draft — self-custody protections retained, federal officials barred from issuing or sponsoring crypto — and senators get one last shot before the August recess.

Elsewhere in regulation: the EU moved to ban crypto platforms in third countries tied to Russian sanctions evasion. The UK and Canada are rolling out tighter identification, tax-reporting and stablecoin rules. Japan has enacted legislation classifying crypto as financial assets, opening the door to Bitcoin ETFs.

The pattern → Four jurisdictions moved the same direction in one fortnight: crypto folded into conventional financial oversight rather than regulated as a separate category. Japan's reclassification is the tell — "financial asset" is a legal status that brings ETFs, custody rules and tax treatment as a package. The era of crypto as regulatory exception is closing, and the price action suggests the market reads that as cost, not legitimacy.

The divergence → "Indian equities are expensive" and "Indian equities are cheap" are both true depending on which index you mean, and the gap between large and mid is the widest it has been in years. That's a 5% recovery of what left.

Build

The stack consolidated; now the argument is about how much it actually helps

Yesterday's top Hacker News post — "2x, not 10x: coding with LLMs in 2026" — argued the 2026 unlock wasn't intelligence but reliability: models crossed the threshold where they can run inside automated feedback loops against objectively checkable criteria. Consequence: further model gains yield diminishing productivity returns, and the remaining upside comes from retooling around what already exists.

July's GitHub Trending corroborates it — dominated by agents rather than models. Coding agents, pentesting agents, trading agents, and the plumbing between them. Notably, narrow single-purpose agent skills climbed the weekly star rankings alongside large platforms, which is what a maturing ecosystem looks like: people assembling specific pieces rather than shopping for platforms.

Three unrelated sources, one direction → the essay, the trending data, and the funding pattern (AI took 65% of a week's Indian startup capital across 27% of deals). The centre of gravity has moved from the model to everything around it. If that holds, returns accrue to whoever builds the best loops on today's models — not to whoever has the best model.

Worth your time, free: if you want to understand agentic systems by building, the highest-leverage skill right now isn't prompting — it's loop design: verification criteria, eval harnesses, feedback structure. Langfuse for observability plus any solid eval harness teaches more than another model comparison. Andrej Karpathy's from-scratch LLM videos remain the best free explanation of the internals that exists.

The Contrarian Corner

Consensus: open-weight models democratise AI and erode the frontier labs' advantage.

The steelman against: Kimi K3 is 1.4TB. Inkling was pretrained on 45 trillion tokens. Running or fine-tuning either requires infrastructure only a few hundred organisations on earth possess. The licence is open; the capability is not. Arguably open-weight releases at this scale strengthen incumbents — they commoditise the model layer, where challengers might have competed, while leaving compute and distribution, where incumbents are unassailable, untouched. Meta's absence from both the open releases and the US framework may be the most informative datapoint of the week. The counter: three years ago the best open model was far behind the best closed one, and that gap has narrowed sharply. Democratisation may simply run on a longer clock than a single week can show.

One Thing to Think About

When something is described as "open," open to whom?

Kimi K3's weights are free to download and effectively impossible for an individual to use. That isn't a contradiction — it's what openness looks like once the artefact gets large enough that access is bounded by capital rather than permission. The same structure shows up everywhere: open data needing infrastructure to query, open standards needing membership fees to influence, open-source software needing full-time staff to deploy. The useful question is never "is this open?" but "what does using it require, and who has that?" Formal access and practical access diverge quietly, and the gap between them is where most power sits.
