The Contrarian Corner

Against: "open weights democratise AI"

Kimi K3 is 1.4TB. Inkling was pretrained on 45 trillion tokens. Running either needs infrastructure a few hundred organisations possess. The licence is open; the capability is not. Arguably mega-scale open releases strengthen incumbents: they commoditise the model layer where challengers might have competed, while leaving compute and distribution — where incumbents are unassailable — untouched. Meta's absence from both the open releases and the US framework may be the week's most informative datapoint.

Counter: the open-closed gap has narrowed sharply over three years. Democratisation may just run on a longer clock than one week shows.

Timeless

Skip "Tragedy of the Commons." Read Ostrom.

Hardin's 1968 essay says shared resources die because individual incentives beat collective interest. Elinor Ostrom spent decades documenting that this is empirically wrong as a general law — Swiss pastures, Japanese fisheries and Spanish irrigation systems self-governed sustainably for centuries — and won a Nobel for it, extracting eight design principles for when self-governance works.

Why today → The AI-governance debate is stuck in Hardin's binary: state control or race to the bottom. Ostrom's finding is a third option that beats both — needing clear boundaries, graduated sanctions, and monitoring by participants. Classified benchmarks fail her first principle before you reach the second.

Rabbit Hole

Nobody knows who wrote the most-deployed code on earth

Critical infrastructure runs on code whose authorship is murky — maintained by one unpaid person or inherited past memory. The canonical image is xkcd 2347: a tower resting on one thin block, "a project some random person in Nebraska has been thanklessly maintaining since 2003."

The bugpocalypse is now hitting well-scrutinised libraries — the ones assumed safe because everyone depends on them. "Many eyes make bugs shallow" was partly a story about code nobody actually read. AI is reading it now. The hole: xkcd 2347 → the 2016 left-pad incident, where unpublishing eleven lines broke half the internet's builds.

One Thing to Think About

When something is called "open" — open to whom?

Kimi K3's weights are free to download and effectively impossible for an individual to use. That's not a contradiction; it's what openness looks like once the artefact is large enough that access is bounded by capital rather than permission. Open data needs infrastructure to query. Open standards need membership fees to influence. Open-source needs staff to deploy. The useful question is never "is this open?" but "what does using it require, and who has that?" Formal and practical access diverge quietly — and the gap is where most power sits.
